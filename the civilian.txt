The days immediately following his strange, hollow audience with Leila were defined by an eerie, suspended quiet. He had been handed a glowing corporate transfer tablet, decorated with the absurd title of Head of Efficiency Policy Implementation, yet the actual summons to pack his meager belongings and move up to the central administrative hub had not yet arrived. Corporate machinery was vast, grinding, and routinely choked by its own inertia; it could take weeks for an authorization code to travel three decks down.

Marek spent those floating, unassigned days back on Rivet-38, reverting entirely to his old habits.

If anyone on the asteroid expected him to put on airs now that he was technically an administrative official, they were severely disappointed. Marek spent most of his afternoons sitting in Coval's cramped, dimly lit quarters. He realised that he spent just as much time here as in his own cabin, and he knew the reason all too well. Seeing Coval after that recent dose of radiation, Marek understood that the old man was nearing the end. With Coval fading, Marek realized he was looking at the absolute last guy from the old pack—a final, fleeting window into a legendary, unbothered past that might slam shut very soon. Marek wasn't one for sentimentality, but he knew opportunities like this were running out; here was a rare chance to relive the past, or at least salvage shreds of it. Meanwhile, Coval had settled into his forced retirement with a peculiar, bitter dignity. Thanks to Betty's furious, unyielding crusade against the local Coalition council, he had officially gotten his shed back.

When Coval was at his lowest—stripped of his status, sleeping on a cot, and deprived of his utility shed along with other privileges—he eventually sat in Betty's cramped kitchen looking pathetically depressed. He offhandedly lamented that losing the shed left him with "nowhere to store his necessary medical equipment". None of the medical equipment even existed, but he was having a point here. Stirring Betty’s existing rage over the pod policy mandate, which eventually made it through asteroid regulations, was like throwing a match into dry oxygen. Her natural fury took over. She turned Coval’s shed, housing, and shift status into her personal holy war, hammering the Coalition administrators until they yielded—completely on her own initiative.

Days went as they usually go... They were playing in the cafeteria this time, observing the board equally as much as the passing people.

They sat across the small metal table, the hexagonal grid board stretched between them as Coval’s lead-weighted miniature tanks systematically dismantled Marek’s forward line. Marek lost almost every single match, but he was finally grasping the game's deeper logic: it wasn't about ingenious, heroic maneuvers, but rather mundane systemic crashing—stripping ability to respond to immediate threat.

This time, a few other old fellows joined them, making for an unusual company. Are they drawn to me? Marek thought for a brief moment.

Coval grunted, tapping a secondary data pad against the table's edge. Even in retirement, he remained obsessively fixated on tracking the sector, brushing aside sanitized official Sol news feeds about "unprecedented logistics optimization" in favor of the dark feeds. He brooded over shadow software repositories, unapproved telemetry scripts, black-market trade flows, and corporate manifests, determined to map the unseen megadeals being struck by rival corporate houses overhead. They argued fervently about where to look. Coval raised a few topics, and a lively debate sparked among the old lads...  just as it always does.

Marek's mind finally detached a bit from space reality; this was just a good wake-up call for him that the world is moving forward with his participation or not, a good call and definitely a well-deserved break from high-stakes stuff. 

Local development on the asteroid belt, however, was becoming as interesting as ever. A new phenomenon was sweeping the lower decks—a viral, pseudo-religious cult known as the 'Angels of the Distant Cloud.' The sect worshipped a massive, interstellar alcohol nebula thousands of light-years away, organizing public gatherings, street performances, and crowdfunding drives to 'fund an expedition to the holy vapor.' Adults laughed at them; children imitated their exaggerated rituals in the corridors. Most people viewed it as harmless, absurd entertainment.

Marek looked at this with duality; he used virtual-reality sensory pills, but more out of biological necessity. Synthetic booze and cheap grain spirits were pure poison. Moreover, they caused inaction; they held you in place, compliant yet strangely satisfied, essentially rotting a person while they lived. As he watched the sect's colorful displays in the commons, yes, he laughed out loud like everybody else, or even more. Those things were entertaining overall, but they were dangerous. He knew, though, he had to chill out—this was no longer space, and he just needed to pull his foot off the gas pedal a bit.

Some other day he stared at the screen and he realized he is finally finding calm; he is unbothered as always.

Feeds in the background flickered with routine disaster reports: a minor thruster explosion on a medium cargo vessel, a costly navigational error that scraped a hull against a shipyard gantry, a commercial collision caused by a sleep-deprived pilot. Standard vacuum hazards. Normal human stupidity.

Even his own recent humiliation-the fact that he had spent weeks believing he was leading a heroic, solitary stand in the sweeper strike, only to realize he had been sitting in a dark cabin with a broken antenna wire-had settled into a quiet, dull ache. It was stinging, yes, to know he hadn't been acknowledged as a striker, but how was that any different from the rest of his life? None of his projects ever worked the way he intended. His tweaks backfired; his calculated risks collapsed into farce; his grand rebellions ended in broken cables. The universe was completely oblivious to his existence, just as it always had been.

It doesn't matter, he told himself, staring at the ceiling of room. Let them ignore me. Eventually, the math will land. Eventually, the universe will have to acknowledge me, even if it's just by accident.

As for Leila... he felt a strange, detached safety regarding her. She was a high-born aristocrat after all, ascending rapidly into the upper stratospheric layers of corporate power. The social distance between them was so vast it functioned as a physical shield. Why would someone of her stature waste time targeting a low-born station rat? She had delivered her cold, devastating speech, unloaded her disgust, and discarded him. Yet he intuitively felt safe from her. The Captain, on the other hand... if the man ever got him in a quiet corridor without corporate escorts present, he would break Marek's bones without a second thought. But Leila was just a politician. Young, volatile, and prone to reckless missteps, she was still predictable at her core—far too self-consumed, chasing nothing beyond power and momentum.

The only real threat to Marek-s peace of mind on the asteroid came from his old neighbors. Word had leaked out that Marek had been awarded a high parade tunic and an administrative title in Fleet Management. Suddenly, people he hadn't spoken to in years were inviting him into their cramped living quarters, offering him lukewarm synthetic tea, and complaining endlessly about the state of Rivet-38-s infrastructure.

Among other things, specifically, they complained about the district sewage and environmental piping network.

"The main sludge lines under Sector 4 have been backing up for six months, Marek," external maintenance workers contracted for the job are cutting corners, and we can't just make necessary modifications. "It's leaking grey water into the air recyclers. We filed eight complaints already. Nothing happens. You're going to Central now. You're the Head of Efficiency! You can fix this with one stroke of a pen." Things like that went on and on.

Marek sat quietly, sipping the terrible tea, listening to them brood. He politely heard them out, nodding reassuringly that he would look into it.

He told them that purely so they would leave him alone and let him have a life. If he admitted he couldn't even understand what they were talking about, what would they say? "By the way, you've had Derek in Central for quite some time"—obviously, Marek knew Derek was useless, but between the two of them, he simply didn't care that much either.

When the official notice of assignment finally arrived, Marek packed his few personal belongings and left the asteroid for the office.

Coval was brooding over recent events—ever since he got sick, he had become way more reflective than usual. I'll squeeze the truth out of you eventually, Coval thought. Wretch as you are. What are you mumbling, you mad fuck? Marek kept acting like he had just been sitting there the whole time—a law-abiding company dog while others were protesting across the sector. Look at you, man: you wouldn't even be capable of working while others are protesting, because you are barely capable of working in the first place. Are you so delusional to think I’d believe that for a second? I need to know the truth, he thought to himself; something odd is happening out there. And even this first event on Samson-47, it smells rotten—nothing adds up, nothing. Surely Marek participated in something important, no matter how much he tries to brush it off as an accident now.

Watching Marek leave Rivet-38 for the office brought a familiar bitterness, but it was twisted up with something much heavier. Typically, when somebody managed to leave Rivet-38, there was that sharp sting of envy Coval knew too well. A few days flew by, and then it was all over—forgotten, swallowed up by station life. But with Marek, it was different. Yeah, Coval felt that injustice of undeserved glory, but something far more overwhelming was happening in his mind.  
A feeling he was sure he had successfully suppressed thirty years ago was beginning to overwhelm him slowly, creeping back up from the deep dark. Because this is life after all: you are born, face reality, get stripped of your fantasies, and eventually you adapt, crawling in the shadows till the very last day of your life—this is how this game should be played. Coval strongly believed that, and this guy dared to defy it, just like that.

Marek was walking into Central, stepping into their polished executive rooms wearing the dust of Rivet-38 on his boots. He was standing right in front of the office building, carrying something heavy and unwritten.

An office job, regardless of how sweeping or grand its title sounded on paper, was completely disconnected from the actual void of space. Having spent most of his life bogged down in administrative friction and desk work, the physical prospect of office labor didn't bother him; he knew the drill all too well. What truly bothered him was the absolute death of potential progress.  

He understood the corporate matrix better than most. Operational space duty and administrative desk tracks were two strictly segregated paths, with a wall built between them that corporate logic almost never crossed. Once a worker was funneled into an office position, standard system rules dictated they would never see the inside of a flight deck or a deep-space vessel again. If normal corporate logic applied, his official career out in space was effectively over.

He desperately searched for an angle, cycling through speculative scenarios until he landed on the semi-independent traders. Those outer-sector crews were notoriously tight-knit, almost never opening their rosters to outsiders—and certainly not to someone of his background. Yet, Marek reasoned, even an independent run occasionally needed someone who understood how the corporate machine worked from the inside—someone who knew how to bypass ticket loops, manipulate administrative handshaking, or simply knew the right person to call.

It was an absurdly far-fetched thought, steeped in pure uncertainty and wild speculation, and he knew it. But as he unpacked his gear, it gave him at least a small, lingering shred of hope to cling to.

His transition from low-born sweeper to Head of Efficiency Policy Implementation was devoid of ceremony. He was handed a keycode to a cramped, windowless office tucked into a dusty sub-deck, furnished with a dented desk, an ancient terminal, and a squeaking chair. In reality, it was just an elegant cubicle and a mandate to complete onboarding. He spent a few days clicking through mind-numbing administrative modules in total isolation. Barely anyone spoke to him, but as he waded through the deluge of system data, he started to build a mental model of the actual information he was interfacing.

First of all, he tried to figure out what his actual position here was, and he started connecting the pieces of information; his position here turned out to be very simple.

Upon reviewing his department charter, Marek discovered the punchline of his new position: he had zero budget, zero staff, and absolute zero real authority.

He was the ghost head of a ghost department. So this is how the implementation of Efficiency-10 looks in practice? he thought, swinging in his chair. Somebody is doing actual work for real, maybe even in this very office, but responsibility apparently lands on him, although he has very little to say.

When Marek realized this, he wasn't disappointed; he was secretly thrilled. It meant he would never be forced to participate in physical crackdowns or sign execution orders for the ice mines. He was completely isolated from the dirty work.  His entire existence was meant to be a decorative buffer-a place where useless paperwork came to die. Honestly speaking, he had anticipated this exact turn of events, so it didn't surprise him in the least. He knew that violent crackdowns and internal security enforcement were the exclusive domain of a completely different department—people Marek had never actually met. In fact, a dark joke circulated among low-born station workers about that outfit: you only ever met them once in your lifetime.
 
He was completing yet another batch of onboarding procedures, which consisted of a few days of clicking through internal fake assignments designed to teach what ticket processing looks like in this system—an activity of clicking Marek thought to himself he had mastered over the years like no other. Okay, it seemed he was ready. He also learned that there were some mandatory meetings, but his calendar was strangely empty; after all, he was the only person on the team. So, he participated only in a few generic, office-wide meetings. After learning how it all worked, he looked at his task list. There were plenty of tasks. Marek noticed they weren't even connected to his actual role, but curiosity drove him forward anyway.
Marek made a genuine effort to complete some tickets: first, to show everyone he took the role seriously; second, out of curiosity about how Central operated; and finally, because it was on his plate right now like everything else in life, so why not just proceed? And the paperwork was indeed a living nightmare. 
In reality, within a few weeks after his appearance and logging into his terminal, Marek's queue was already flooded with hundreds of automated administrative tickets. He had an assistant, obviously, who allowed him to understand the actual meaning of the tasks, but even this didn't change the reality: tasks were not exciting. After a few weeks working on them, Marek noted they fell cleanly into two distinct, horrifying categories:

Type 1 Tickets: Micro-discrepancy resolution files. These required Marek to open hundreds of pages of fragmented, conflicting digital archives covering years of discrepancies, comparing obscure line items like vacuum-seal serial numbers or vintage lubricant requisitions across multiple unindexed databases. It was mind-numbing, eye-bleeding labor meant for absolute lunatics. The goal was to detect and fix actual discrepancies, and there were plenty, by the way. Sometimes entire sections of incompatible documents were meant to describe the same thing; it was a completely agonizing chore to merge them.

Type 2 Tickets: These were procedural tickets governed by "Paragraph 22." Ticket A would schedule Meeting B to discuss an efficiency discrepancy. Meeting B would defer the issue to Committee C. Committee C would suspend all action on the ticket because Meeting A had failed to issue a preliminary quorum report—which, as Marek noted, was specifically left suspended in mid-air waiting for Meeting B's rollout. Or similar scenarios in different orders.

Before Marek lost his initial spark, he was actually making progress and knocking out a few tickets. But it didn't take long for the numbness to start creeping in. He could already see where this was heading: another six months of this mind-numbing corporate rot, and he’d go completely nuts, left sitting there genuinely cursing the day he was ever born.

To make matters worse, comms channels dropped daily administrative updates—massive, system-wide 'Policy Explosions.' Mid-level management was constantly merging low-level regulations, scrapping operational frameworks, and swapping procedural nomenclature. Next to nothing ever actually changed on the ground, but station staff were forced to spend hundreds of hours constantly migrating data from one arbitrary folder layout to another. This was extremely annoying: if you bogged down in resolving one complex issue for too long, the universe just moved under your feet and you were merging into the void. Marek hated the feeling. The sheer amount of information he consumed daily in those days was overwhelming; he looked around at his coworkers and started to understand more than ever why white-collar office workers are oftentimes seen as soulless. It was as if this was by the very design of the workplace and how it captures all cognitive capabilities—stealing almost all of it so that besides crushing feelings, grievances, and hate, there was not much space for anything more.

Eventually, Marek gave up trying to follow the policy updates after his third month. He sat in his squeaking chair, staring at the glowing monitor, utterly convinced that the entire corporate bureaucracy was being run by clinically insane people.

"If I try to do this work," Marek thought, watching the ticket counter tick upward, "I will go mad within the next few months." The only way to survive this place is to be so spectacularly, uncomfortably useless that they regret ever giving me a desk. Marek, with ever-decreasing passion, mastered tackling new cohorts of pending tasks; he noticed that his engagement dwindled. Instead, he started turning his attention to the more general setup of the place. He listened to gossip for quite some time, and he started to build a mental picture of the place from this sparse information, especially now that he had started disengaging from the daily chores of task processing.

Outside his tiny office, the broader political landscape of the sector was crystallizing in his mind. The sector was locked in an intense, silent duopoly between two powerful corporate families: the Houston-Leila faction (which controlled station sanitation, sweeper fleets, and cleaning protocols) and another family (which controlled heavy transport quality, corridor transit rights, and throughput metrics). Marek learned that the Houstons control many sectors completely, but Corporate wants the families to share competencies in some places—this is one of them. Because Marek had been decorated alongside Leila, he was universally tagged by middle management as a "Houston dog," like almost everybody in this particular office.  

Regarding the actual strike, Marek noticed something even more interesting.

Corporate PR had successfully spun the recent sweeper strike as mere 'implementation friction'—claiming a small group of conservative, outdated workers had struggled to adapt to new, highly advanced tracking software. Reprisals had been carried out quietly behind closed doors; and because they have to keep the pretense that it was a minor efficiency misunderstanding and glitch, they were surprisingly benign. Out of two thousand sweepers, fifty were sent to ice, but mostly those who actively resisted arrest. Two to three hundred were suspended temporarily or shuffled around, meaning that maybe 15% were directly removed from operations—a surprising development, but efficient and logical, thought Marek.

Whenever Marek saw the actual number of people sent to ice scraping and the automated reassignment logs flicker across the central feed, he suffered cold, uncomfortable recollections of his own near-disastrous last stand. It wasn't the most brilliant action he ever tried to trigger, he assessed.

"Okay, things are starting to get clearer, but I am here and it is time to wander around a bit," he thought. Instead of doubling down on pretending he was working at his cubicle, he actually took a walk around the office—and he started doing this more and more frequently.

Marek noticed that the horrors people faced were more or less the same everywhere, with the only difference being that those with larger teams were tied up in 'high-stakes' meetings every day—you could even visibly tell how some of them were bracing for an individual stand. But other than that, it was identical across the board: people grinding away in cubicles, trying to resolve ridiculous tickets, wearing gray faces as if the work itself were targeting them personally. Marek felt it too; he understood perfectly why they looked the way they did. Then a funny reflection struck him: it was all a bit of an arms race. If someone processed discrepancy issues too quickly, it sent shockwaves through the system, forcing everyone else to synchronize their own discrepancies, software, and legislative compliance packages. The bloat and quagmire were entirely self-propelling.

But there was something truly extraordinary he had noticed even on his first day of work, though he had completely dismissed it back then. There was an Oasis of Peace on the bottom floor of the building—a place where they handed out biscuits, tea, and artificial honey that was presumably chemically identical to the original from Earth. Over time, he started spending more and more of his day hanging around there. On paper, it was just a break area meant for receiving visitors and handling semi-official business, but he began to notice a core group of people who were constantly present—the exact same faces over and over. He realized the breakdown of the office: 95% of the personnel were grinding away trying to resolve tickets, while 5% seized every opportunity to hang out downstairs with biscuits, tea, and synthetic honey. So there is a place where I belong in this building, he thought. Unlike his other coworkers, these people seemed much more like his kind of crowd.

He also caught a glimpse of Derek from his own asteroid. He was definitely among the slackers; he had probably already realized that one needs to have some social capital and momentum to be promoted, and he was notorious even on the asteroid—even Marek, detached as he was, knew that. Marek pretended that he did not recognize the guy, and he noticed with satisfaction that the feeling was absolutely mutual.

Right now, something different got his attention. He started to learn gossip about the company here, more effectively than anywhere else. That is how he learned, for example, that the guy responsible for pushing Efficiency-10 in the first place was initially removed, supposedly, but later Marek learned that he was assigned a different position in House Houston's internal structure in a different sector. And this position wasn't even lower—it was higher; he was demoted by promotion, but strictly within the internal circles of family operations.
He learned that the quality of vacuum was degraded by one level, and the transport family was not happy about that, because certain lucrative transits require higher quality, etc.—nuances, but pretty useful to know, thought Marek.

Funny thing was, plenty of the people hanging out down there spent their whole time complaining about virtually everything. Marek caught himself thinking, Man, how can you sit there criticizing everything while stuffing your face with free biscuits? Maybe instead of slacking off for hours every single day, you should actually do some work instead of whining all the time.

Eventually, even the office building was not enough and he was prowling around the vicinity. Fortunately for him, in close proximity there was a port and various corporate and semi-independent maintenance facilities for ship equipment. Initially, he was just wandering—like most of the time, just wandering—observing their procedures and operations. Eventually, he was able to strike up a conversation here and there, which was probably hilarious considering he was wearing a corporate uniform. Things like that happen, obviously, but not on a daily basis and not spontaneously; he couldn't help it, though—he was just too interested in their operations. He learned some details about the technical side and a bit about the actual workflow; at this point, he was able to conceptualize what major and minor work was being done at any given moment in those facilities. He did all this during actual working hours, but strangely nobody noticed. Okay, some coworkers looked with suspicion at him once in a while; clearly they were perplexed why Marek was disappearing for hours instead of sitting in his cubicle, but nobody said a word.

Yet once in a while, Marek would suffer a sudden outburst of guilt over avoiding his duties. Overcome by a pang of conscience, he would retreat to his desk and force himself to grind away at his work for two or three days straight.
Without fail, however, those brief sprints ended the same way. He was immediately reminded of why he was avoiding the job in the first place: he fundamentally hated it. The mind-numbing, bureaucratic busywork revolted him.

The shipyard inspired him, though; he ordered a few electronic components and started tinkering with simple radio designs. It turned out to be his favorite discipline right now, but strangely, at work he was just not feeling it. Yes, he had time, but it was just misplaced, so he resorted to a slow-burning hobby activity after work.

A few months passed and he understood the ropes, but he still knew little about the actual state of Efficiency-10's implementation. He realized one thing clearly: they were keeping him idling in place for a single reason—to have a head ready for the chopping block if things went south. So, it was time to really audit how things were actually going.

Efficiency-10 was split into two phases. The Phase 1 software rollout had already happened, but the accompanying 10% efficiency gain was recognized internally as a rushed failure. This forced them to relabel it as 'massive deployment friction,' though that was only scratching the surface. As the Company scrambled into crisis management mode, Corporate built a 'reconciliation package' of temporary fixes to smooth over the damage. This was Phase 2, and it was way messier.

On paper, Marek was responsible for overall implementation; neither Phase 1 nor Phase 2 belonged to him, but lower-tier clerks—including (who else) Derek!—did the actual work for Phase 2 at least. Marek initially marvelled at their inexperience, but quickly grasped the predatory corporate logic: force green officials into making unpopular or illegal choices, stripping them of peer goodwill while handing executive management permanent legal leverage to ensure their absolute loyalty in the long run.

Phase 2 had procedural, technical, and reactive-compliance enforcement components. Security handled the heavy lifting—imposing competence and streamlining the conservative, incapable human factor—while the remaining sub-phases crawled along. If those were ever concluded, a trial and assessment would follow, and that final assessment was critical to Marek's fate.

There was also an interesting caveat attached to Phase 2: Corporate retained the right to impose temporary measures—essentially emergency, sector-wide laws designed to sweep away protest fallout briskly and frictionlessly. These were typically reverted once things stabilized, granting them a convenient, half-legal status that allowed management to pull whatever stunt they wanted. Responsibility for these measures fell on a mere handful of people—a random group of five or six guys in the office. They hadn't actually done much with that power, as Marek knew from being in the loop, he was legally required to give his stamp of approval anyway—a stamp that, naturally, was considered granted by default.

At the end of the day, Corporate would eventually roll out Phase 2—the combined technical hardware overhaul and procedural updates. Once deployed, they would run an official assessment across one or two sweeping cycles, ultimately stamping the entire Efficiency-10 policy as either a success or a failure.

That assessment period was the exact moment Marek was actually needed for. He sat in his office, quietly waiting on the virtual chopping block, positioned perfectly to take the executioner's blow if the metrics collapsed.

The dark irony of his situation was that, on paper, Marek looked like a rising star within House Houston.

He learned the most likely reason why he was picked for the job in the first place. Officially, he hadn't participated in the strike—he had remained loyal—but that was far too irrelevant. There was another minor detail, though.

It was an absurd technological glitch. After the Samson-47 disaster, Marek had been the only person on board awarded two official corporate commendations—one for Valor, and another for Technical Competence. Though the ribbons were functionally worthless, corporate database sorting algorithms often automatically ranked personnel by total award count.

Whenever an executive or HR system pulled up the Samson-47 file, the search algorithm routinely floated Marek’s name straight to the top of the feed. He was consistently listed above all ordinary low-born crew, and occasionally even ranked above high-born aristocrats who had only received a single honor.

To any executive casually skimming corporate metrics, Tech-3 Marek appeared to be a loyal, rapidly ascending white-knight asset—making him the absolute perfect high-profile scapegoat to be sacrificed the moment anything went wrong.

For other elements of Phase 2, the outlook was actually bleak.

The second phase—a hardware overhaul—stalled entirely as design bureaus and subcontractors viciously fought over manufacturing scraps. Marek was kept out of those negotiations, but he heard they were bogging down and that the fight over scraps was fierce. On another note, he did have clearance for the procedural committee drafting new sweeper regulations—essentially the legal part of the Phase 2 package.

When Marek attended a session, the twenty committee members initially listened intently to him and even began discussing his remarks right after he spoke from real field experience. Within just a few minutes, however, the conversation completely diverged from the topics he had raised. He tried to steer them back a few times to no avail. Knowing that the sweepers would just ignore these unworkable manuals when necessary anyway, Marek finally stopped attending.

There were other status meetings involving management—concerning the progress of new procedures—but those were a separate beast entirely, and scarce anyway. Marek still attended them, but because they never asked him directly for anything, he didn't even bother preparing.

There was one other interesting part: Phase 1 had rolled out, and when Marek looked at the sweepers numbers, they actually looked good.

This led to his first major realization—he had stumbled upon the Sweeper Math Paradox.

Across the sector after the strike broke, the remaining sweepers were pulling genuinely impressive figures. Management attributed this entirely to the Phase 1 software rollout, using the success to reinforce their universal belief that the update alone was great and that those obstinate, obsolete workers had just rebelled for no reason. Marek, however, knew the real explanation behind the phenomenon.

When he pulled up the performance metrics of the remaining sweeper fleet, the math was stark, elegant, and completely counterintuitive: removing three hundreds sweepers had actually increased individual sweeping efficiency across the sector. With fewer ships crammed into the corridors, sector overlap dropped drastically. The remaining sweepers had 15% more breathing room, meaning they weren't constantly sweeping over each other's fields or fighting for the same scrap clusters. Individual quotas were suddenly much easier to hit, even though the total volume of debris cleared across the entire sector was slightly lower.

Because here lay, in a nutshell, the fundamental absurdity of Efficiency-10. Leila had specifically tweaked the system to target individual efficiency metrics rather than the overall quality of the vacuum in the sector. She designed it this way because sector vacuum quality was already pristine; the only metric left with any room for 'improvement' on paper was individual efficiency—and to target Marek personally, that was more than enough.

Corporate never bothered to change the actual premise. They could have restated the mission to say, "We will make the space even cleaner," but instead, they stuck strictly to individual numbers. In truth, those metrics were nothing more than a leash around the sweepers' necks—a completely irrelevant tool of reprisal.

Cleaning space is ultimately a zero-sum game. Sure, new debris gets generated once in a while, but overall, if you increase individual efficiency, the available scrap drops until there is simply little left to pick up. The management ignored this basic reality. Marek knew that because of this obsession with individual figures, as long as space remained saturated with debris—which it was right now—the numbers would look great. The other family obviously didn't like that detail, but Marek certainly did.

The purge had caused orbital space quality in the primary transit corridor to drop by one full safety level. Debris was accumulating, slowing down heavy freighters. This was not the problem for Houston, but for their partner family in the sector, it was. Normally, their efforts go hand in hand, but here, at least regarding Efficiency-10, conflict was obvious. They waited for now, giving Houstons time to sort out the mess, but eventually, they will escalate the issue no matter what.

Being completely stripped of power and influence, Marek felt deeply uneasy. He was stuck playing a waiting game for the final assessment, and seeing how sluggishly the procedural committee was moving, he knew the outcome wouldn't be pretty.

There was an intriguing angle to it, though. He had already established that the teams behind those temporary measures were tiny—literally two or three people each—and though various groups held different levels of power, Derek happened to be one of them.

As this clicked, a plan started forming in Marek's head: if he could figure out how to maintain the status quo for as long as possible, they might just manage to wrap up the formal bureaucracy right on time. 

He and Derek had obviously noticed each other, but up until now they had not directly interacted. But now Marek had ideas, and this meant he finally needed to strike up a conversation. Derek and the other guys were part of the biscuit party, and Marek started developing a plan on how to handle them.

Whenever Derek walked up to the honey stand to grab his afternoon break, Marek would suddenly appear out of nowhere. Marek would step awkwardly into Derek's personal space, aggressively drizzle synthetic honey onto a dry biscuit, and begin verbally badgering him in a low, intense whisper.

"I am disappointed about the quality of the sweepers," he would constantly start off. "More of them are of shady background and ready to rebel again. What do you think?"

Derek apparently thought the exact same way about Marek, but eventually he agreed, and from that point on, it became their main topic of conversation. The dislike Derek fostered toward Marek grew immensely; he started getting nervous whenever he saw Marek in plain view, but obviously there was no way he could avoid interaction.

"The pipeline is too soft; we need a more conservative approach. I know this is temporary, but those guys are a real danger. I know them personally."  This, Derek believed very easily.  "We need to mandate elevated field security screenings for all new applicants. They are also being trained improperly—damaging expensive corporate hardware."

He went on like that over and over again.
Within a week, Marek's relentless cafeteria stalking drove Derek into a state of severe clinical paranoia. Whenever Derek saw Marek shuffling down a corridor, he would visibly flinch, lock his cubicle door, sprint into an elevator, or frantically delete his unread email queue to avoid any digital connection to the strange, wandering slacker. But eventually, Derek caved; they passed a new law because having a good time during tea and biscuits was just too important.

It was part of the temporary measures, but it looked solid. It was assessed that discontent was caused by the proliferation of unrecommended elements in the workplace cleaner crews, which was blatantly dangerous in light of the fact that they operated expensive equipment. So, the required security clearance was elevated by one level, meaning a much longer hiring process. Additionally, as a backdoor proposal, they extended mandatory training while security clearance was ongoing, so sweepers got something out of it.
Marek mastered his corner-and-force approach. He was after some other guys now, but they yielded much faster. Now another regulation was in place: best talents are picked and sent for further training for telecom or transport operations. It was disguised as a leaders' sieve. Marek was a bit clever here; he remembered from protests that actual leaders were rarely the guys who were working the most effective way—those were oftentimes not liked that much, seen as competitors, but nevertheless, in corporate eyes, good work means good at everything: leading people, etc., or eventual protests. 
Removing those guys helped keep efficiency numbers better for the remaining guys. But it also gave hope that if you work hard, there could be a promotion awaiting—yes, this was temporary and such decisions are definitely outside of offices like that, but within the package, it could go.


One day, another issue dropped into his lap.
As he routinely scanned his incoming messages, one immediately caught his attention—it had originated from outside the office.

It was partially encrypted and apparently untraceable. He clicked to see what it was all about. It contained a raw set of a few images showing him inside the cockpit of a sweeper ship, accompanied by a short message stating that the sender had more information about his activities during the sweeping operation. There were a few links to secure, encrypted channels. Marek knew how to access them, but he hadn't installed the proper software on his workstation. He was slightly curious, but he ultimately thought better of it. It was a classic blackmail attempt—an anonymous threat to expose him, perhaps as a strike sympathizer.

Marek stared at the glowing blackmail message for three seconds.

Then he deleted it.

He simply didn't care. Installing the software was way too much hassle, and he was completely detached. If Corporate PR found out, they would bury the file anyway to avoid undermining their own narrative about the strike. Besides, since he was the one in charge of Efficiency-10 here, there was no way it would surface in any meaningful way—at least not right now. Even if something actually came of it, he would just be quietly fired... which was fine, considering he was finished with office work regardless. The blackmail was utterly useless against a man who had no remaining ambitions left to destroy.

Still, he sat down at his terminal to set up a custom email filter; he had no desire to see the message pop up again. The process turned out to be an infuriating chore. Marek brooded over the interface, marveling at how modern corporate software could feature ten thousand useless administrative bells and whistles while making a fundamental task like blocking an address absurdly convoluted. He was forced to consult his system assistant, and even after tweaking the rules, he wasn't entirely sure he had configured the filter correctly.
As he fiddled with the settings, he couldn't help analyzing the technical side of the threat. The sender claimed to possess some data from his vessel. That alone was bizarre. When Marek had disengaged his systems after his last flight, he had performed a clean manual shutdown—a procedure that purged local active logs. Pulling back those data was no easy feat; high-end capture hardware and specialized extraction software—the kind of either military-grade or sophisticated shadow software tradecraft Coval used to talk about—had to be used. Someone had used serious equipment to pull those files. He had to be a technician; it could be a grievance against Corporate after the crackdown; there were rumors that there was a bunch of rebels who secretly tried to undermine Corporate—after all, this software alone was proof of that.
The irony of the situation wasn't lost on him. In the eyes of Corporate, Marek was the strike-breaker who had received a promotion. Now he stood in an executive office tunic, yet some anonymous activist was targeting him as if he were a key corporate asset. In the eyes of this blackmailer, he was the exact corporate scum which should be targeted and wiped out, and there was some sympathy to this. Obviously, people with such a level of grievances were notoriously naive and easy to manipulate, but still, he couldn't blame the poor lad—he needed to just learn how the world operates, that's all.
A few days later, another notification pinged in his inbox from the same address.
Marek sighed. So much for setting up the anti-spam filter.
He clicked the message anyway, careful not to touch the embedded link. The body of the text was explicit:

'Remember me, Marek? It's Stephan, your pal from the cleaning fleet. I have materials that might interest you. And btw, it is weird that the guy who was actively participating in the strike—which I was able to observe live from my cockpit—is enjoying his peace in an office while others like you are rotting in radiation pits, weird isn't it?'

It wasn't a random anti-corporate hacktivist airing systemic grievances. It was Stephan. Which meant this wasn't political—it was intensely personal.

Stephan implied that he had definitive proof Marek had participated in the strike. Sitting back in his chair, the whole picture immediately clicked into place for Marek. During those final, desperate days of the sweeping cycle, Stephan had been operating right near Marek’s sector boundary, competing for the exact same scrap cluster. Even with Marek’s transponder cable knocked loose and his main drive idle, the cavity in which the signal attenuated was emitting some level of radiation outside of the hull in the proximity of the ship. There was no escape from that physical reality; the signal was distorted and the patrol unit misread it, interpreting it as gibberish since they were at a huge distance initially. More importantly, they didn't have anything more than basic protocol, which apparently was not able to decode this mess. But Stephan was nearby all the time, so what was unrecognizable for the fleet was not for Stephan. Stephan’s precise, by-the-book scanning arrays had picked up those low-level energy emissions and read 'strike message'.
From Stephan’s perspective, Marek hadn't been sitting in a dark cabin agonizing over a loose wire. Marek had deliberately gone dark, parked his vessel alongside the strikers, and defied Corporate like anybody else—he was a legitimate participant. It meant that Marek wasn't actually totally invisible! Stephan had watched him "strike" with his own eyes.  Marek understood Stephan's bitter logic perfectly. Stephan was the ultimate rule-abiding corporate suck-up. He barely cut corners, he followed every protocol, and when the strike hit, he stayed on duty, fully expecting Corporate to crush the rebels and reward his unwavering loyalty. Instead, the strike ended, and Marek—the lazy, corner-cutting slacker who Stephan saw had actively mutinied to the very end—was plucked from his cockpit and promoted to Head of Efficiency, while Stephan was left drifting in the vacuum, forgotten.
Stephan harbored a genuine, burning grievance. Marek recognized the danger: someone with that level of self-righteous, toxic resentment was entirely unpredictable. Negotiating with a snake like Stephan was out of the question—Marek had no intention of offering credits or playing into his blackmail. He would block the threat cold.
Yet beneath his pragmatic annoyance, Marek felt a strange, unexpected surge of quiet pride. It was absurd, but it was profoundly refreshing. Corporate viewed Marek as a harmless, loyal slacker. The strike organizers viewed him as a nobody. But to Stephan—the guy who did everything by the book—Marek was a full-blown, legitimate protester all the time who had played the corporate system and defied authority.
Stephan’s toxic obsession gave Marek something he hadn't expected: genuine validation. In the eyes of at least one person in the solar system, Marek hadn't just been a foolish idiot trapped in a dark room with a broken antenna. He had been a real striker. Even if Corporate suppressed the logs, and even if the situation threatened to backfire on him later, Stephan’s furious hatred confirmed that Marek’s presence had left a mark.
Okay, he would block the guy, but the mood was strangely lighthearted for Marek.
For the rest of the day, that strange, overwhelming sense of secret triumph stayed with him, hanging warm and heavy in the quiet of his office. 

Initially, Marek wanted to opt out of Rivet-38’s petty demands entirely. I can’t do anything anyway, he thought, and what’s in it for me? Making a vague promise to look into the matter was just a polite gesture to keep the peace, but now the issue was slowly eating at him. At first, he planned to brush them off by saying he’d raised their concerns to the committee—that Central was passionately working on it despite the bureaucratic hurdles—but the issue kept bouncing around in his head. Eventually, he started poking into various station problems, only to find himself dragged into the sewage piping disaster.

Ultimately, Marek figured out why the sewage pipe had never been fixed. It wasn’t a technical issue or a lack of budget; it was pure bureaucratic paralysis. The system called it governance; he called it Complex Handshaking. On paper, repairs required a simple sequence: Resource Allocation (Party A) issued a request, and Facility Maintenance (Party B) handled the work. But after an accident years ago, the process was tweaked. Once Party B finished, Party A had to run a post-audit, exposing Party B to strict liability in case of negligence. To cover themselves, Maintenance demanded a mountain of preliminary paperwork before even taking the ticket—a demand Allocation fiercely resisted. Countless tickets stalled at Party B, who claimed there wasn't enough documentation for safe execution, while Party A refused to budge. Simple procedures dragged on indefinitely. Repairs that should have taken three days rotted for decades while grey water seeped into the air recyclers. Marek wondered if there was a subtle corporate logic behind the gridlock—a cold method of population control. Keeping station dwellers trapped in endless micro-crises over backed-up toilets and leaking valves kept them constantly occupied with trivia. Exhausted people don't organize. Yet whenever Corporate needed something—a new cargo vault or security grid—the system was never a problem. They simply sent in legal enforcers who steamrolled regulations and finished the job in twenty-four hours.

This is part of something bigger, Marek thought. On the surface, it made sense: a past tragedy caused by real negligence had killed people, so the system reacted. The same kind of people now sitting on these procedural committees for sweepers had gathered to figure out how to avoid the next disaster. Although Marek hated their methods, he knew they were genuinely engaged and passionate about their work. Some of these office workers are so obsessed with their tickets they probably regret going home at the end of the day, he noted. They were completely unbothered by—and oblivious to—the fact that most of their work was formal compliance and paperwork cleanup with minimal real-world impact. As he spent more time analyzing these handshaking bottlenecks, Marek realized how these committees built up a thick sediment of policies, patents, and regulations over time. Accumulating for decades, the whole process eventually became pure theater, as no one even remembered the original root cause.

Human life was apparently so precious, yet Marek knew there was no turning back from this setup. The only real fix would be the luxury of allowing people to die once in a while—a compromise Corporate would never allow. Instead, you were granted the freedom to rot in the shadows, because in a system of forced inaction, that was the default state.

He already hated playing the cynical sociopath against Derek and the other stability enforcement officers. He felt it shouldn't be done under any circumstances, yet he rationalized it again: 'I have no choice. These motherfuckers owe me for what I am about to do,' he thought, referring to his fellow asteroid dwellers. So he set out to 'fix' the pipeline problem, tracking down the responsible guys and nagging them relentlessly. Though just as exhausted as the poor bastards he was hounding, Marek eventually pushed the issue through. But he swore to himself that this was the end. He would tell the people back home that he raised the issue to the committee, and that an automated system had finally resolved the piping problem—otherwise, they’d flood him with endless new requests. Still, he felt a quiet satisfaction in knowing he had delivered on his promise.

Left to his own devices once more, Marek wandered through the building, pulling an ordinary ticket now and then, but spending most of his time around the workshops. People there were finally starting to recognize him; after all, he was more than capable of holding a competent technical discussion, and often did. He also learned that while these workshops were semi-independent on paper, corporate patronage was practically mandatory. He had already suspected as much. On top of that, internal guilds, associations, and unwritten rules made the crowd a pretty exclusive club. Still, Marek's restless mind was already beginning to spot something new—a faint light at the end of a long, dark tunnel.

But there was also another thing which started recently: he was already here for more than six months and he started to notice a subtle shift, one which promised difficulties. Marek immediately spotted the trap being laid for his department.

The transport family was aggressively pushing a new motion: they demanded the immediate, mass hiring of a few hundred new sweeper operators to clean the corridors. It had to come to this eventually. Transfer quality degraded; initially, it could be explained by efficiency friction, but not forever, and degraded space meant some lucrative transports couldn't pass through. They wanted a simple thing: a mass rehire of sweepers, basically dropping all temporary measures inspired by Marek, reducing security clearance for new sweepers, as well as stopping sending the best sweepers to academia for telecom and transport. This was an inevitable and logical step—he knew it all too well—but it would degrade quotas: many inexperienced sweepers cramped in a small area meant a significant drop in efficiency on paper. Even if efficiency rolled out soon, it would be a disaster.

Moreover, everybody in the office who was involved seemed unbothered, not understanding the gravity of the situation. Marek completely lost hope that the procedural committee would conclude its work anytime soon. The technical branch finally settled on contractors—at least most of them—but Marek knew it takes time. This meant one thing: the transport guys will push while Efficiency-10 stalls completely.

As a result, he will be cannibalized, pushed onto the chopping block, and 'executed.' Okay, this is not the same as 'ice'; they will simply let him go, but Marek started to have various interesting projects and ideas regarding his future, especially while walking around the port and maintenance/workshop facilities near it. The last thing he wanted was to allow himself to be let go in disgrace back to the asteroid now.

On the other hand, his hand was empty of cards—it always was. Okay, right now Efficiency-10, which is ongoing, looks great; they are hitting the target 10% with some reserve, but he knew in current dynamics it will collapse within 6 months, definitely 12, when they cram space with newbies.

There was only one card he could play right now, and while it was improbable at the time, he somehow took a liking to the idea: he will simply write what is going on on the ground to somebody from the Houston family—after all, he belongs to the family now. And it seems that he knows only one person. She will dismiss the message for sure, but Marek will try. He wanted to have a clear conscience, so he wrote as clearly as possible why Efficiency-10 works right now, what measures keep it working like hiring freezes and promotions. Moreover, he wrote about the immense pressure to fix space by hiring more personnel, which is a nail in the coffin under any circumstances... He wrote about his frustration regarding delays, etc. At the very end, he simply proposed rolling out the concluding phase right now, starting the audit period, and harvesting the success. This will be beneficial to both of them, and this is what matters. Obviously, over time the situation dilutes, but the paper trail is what matters.

He sent the message. He was not hoping for anything, but he had done his part; his conscience was clean.

A few days later something extraordinary was happening. Extreme stir among the procedure committee; apparently there is a decision from the very top. Schedules have been modified: they do not have 1 year, they are having like 2 weeks, and the technical side was also stripped out of scope and downsized extremely. Marek understood in seconds what is happening: a change of direction, and no way he was not influencing these decisions. Frustration and stir were pleasant to watch; Marek was so proud of himself. After 2 weeks the procedure was complete, after 2 months the technical part was rolling out, and soon after trial and assessment started. Moreover, hiring stalled and promotions continued. It was being said among the biscuit party that Efficiency-10 has to conclude, and quick, and it has to be successful. Families struck a deal for some reason.

And that is how it went. Months passed, trials concluded, and crews achieved a 10–15% boost in efficiency. Sector quality was still below expectations, but this was attributed to early phases and previous difficulties; overall, a blatant success. A few months passed and the final assessment went through, and even Marek was writing some final remarks from his perspective. By the way, during the last month he worked very little; he even started to play Coval's game with the security guard. Oftentimes he just spent time among the biscuit party guys, but most of the time he was walking outside of the building. He knew he had won this time.

After some time he was named among others as a father of success in an official memo. This was good because he knew that this gives him some space to negotiate his future, but definitely he will not stay in this office much longer no matter what. He needs to wait a few weeks and there will be a decision, and then who knows.

The rollout was also favorable for Leila—having been a little bit tied to this issue, now they could move her to another outpost. Having spearheaded the software rollout, she scored a massive political victory, securing her promotion to an elite corporate division in the inner Sol systems. Incredible what just happened regarding this cleaners trouble, but fortunately it is all over.

An absolute failure of an office worker who spends his shifts eating stolen biscuits in the cafeteria, babbling gibberish to middle managers, and hanging out with low-born mechanics in the shipyards because he lacks the discipline for administrative duty.

Okay, we have done what he stated. We struck a deal, rewards are being given, my daughter is finally off the hook. I am glad that it all happened this way.
