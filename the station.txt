


The Boundary Rail Hub at Grid 9-4 wasn’t a grand orbital habitat or a bustling transit port. It was a glorified secondary relay—a cold, low-mass outpost bolted directly into the cracked crust of Callisto’s outermost ice-and-rock ridge. In the cold hierarchy of corporate logistics, the station barely registered as a named asset on spreadsheets. It existed solely as a low-capacity bridge to anchor local magnetic guide-rails, gather backup sensor data, and catch faint deep-space signals that main relays deemed too low-priority to process.
Operating the station was an exercise in automated persistence, yet for Marek, the base had simply become "home" for quite some time now. Here he had his quarters; here his entire universe had revolved for the past few months.

He was walking in a suit around the establishment. The station maintained a twenty-kilometer stretch of ground-based magnetic guide-rails that steered uncrewed heavy ore sleds through Callisto's cratered terrain toward equatorial cargo docks. Power came from a small, buried atomic generator, sending steady magnetic fields through the track to keep cargo carts aligned against the moon's uneven gravity. And man, on this moon there is a hell of radiation and electromagnetics—between Jupiter's field, the cosmic background, and our own reactors, it's everywhere. They are not saying this out loud, but surely such an amount of electromagnetics constitutes the real reason why financial compensation is so decent here. For me, it is not such a great deal, but for guys in their 30s, it may as well be, because they are not here only for glory. Those contracts are lucrative, and if you manage to return home one day, it makes a difference, even in our rigid society. Yet if you only manage to live another five or ten years after returning, trading your lifespan for high pay, how does all that money actually benefit you in the end?

He walked straight into the middle of the small space dock. As he looked across the port, his own voyage sprang to mind, and his thoughts drifted back to the time right before he arrived.

It was quite a story, because he got here on an actual passenger ship, although most of the way he was put to sleep; overall, for two months he walked around the hull. At the start of those two months, though, his access was limited. The first couple of days he just stayed in his cabin, completely OK with that, but increasingly he started to poke around, and a few times they explicitly forbade him further access, restricting him to only a few quarters. But apparently a maintenance officer, maybe driven by some circulating stories about Marek, offered him the opportunity to solve one problem, and Marek agreed.

This was an extremely convoluted emergency shutdown happening once in a while in some obscure system on the ship. Logs were immense, and familiarity with the internal workings of the system was required. He was allowed because technically Marek was not a passenger; he was a temporary crew member. But apparently they kept him asleep most of the journey just in case he got too serious about the job, he thought.

The problem was basically unsolvable, but Marek didn't bother. He dived in for a few days, demonstrating engagement. He built trust, and this way he was able to penetrate some places in the hull which he, as a temporary crew member, should have been allowed to explore in the first place. And he was blown away. He obviously didn't understand how it all worked, but man, this was like 100 years in the future—everything so well thought out, brilliant design. He knew that passenger ships were this way, but the contrast to the cargo vessel was staggering.

He divided his time: once in a while he tried to tackle the issue, although he already knew it was well beyond his knowledge, preparation, and likely his capabilities. The core problem was buried under endless, random logs that obscured any real information, and his understanding of the system was nonexistent. He never admitted how lost he actually was; as long as he played the game, he was a member of the crew. But instead of wasting energy on an unsolvable task, he played games on his pod while pretending to fervently analyze convoluted diagnostics. In truth, he preferred the tangible stuff—walking the deck and reverse-engineering how the station’s components actually worked by inspecting system readings and operations.

When the travel was about to conclude, the maintenance chief with exaggerated surprise noted that he couldn't solve such a trivial problem. Marek silently admitted defeat, but overall he was equally satisfied. The alternative would have been to stay in the passenger quarters for the entire journey.

Now he was walking into the warehouse area, looking out on his beloved kingdom: a simplified personal railway built to facilitate scientific activities—placing and maintaining a network of thousands of sensors spread across miles of terrain.

From the get-go, Marek had received quite an exciting assignment: planning and executing the end-to-end development of those rails alongside sensor positioning and maintenance. Initially, Marek couldn't believe his eyes when given this responsibility, but here, where the station crew was sparse and facility priority was low, they didn't have to deal with bureaucracy or committees.

He recalled how his work started: surveying the equipment in the warehouse and the current state of the network. It was decent, but he immediately spotted potential for growth, with plenty of room for improvement where blank spots existed in the sensor coverage. He started right away, learning how to operate the robots, carts, systems, and planning software.

The work was staggering in scope, but he wasn't feeling faint-hearted this time. After a few weeks, he was out on the actual edge with his robots, laying track, configuring sensory networking protocols, and analyzing radio emission schedules, noise parameters, and interference.

Watching another batch of rails being laid out on the preplanned track, he observed the robots going back with the cart into the warehouse.

The rails were lightweight, but even so, he could carry only so much in one go. Fortunately, he was able to successfully set up a robotic queue, and the robots were taught to make round trips gathering rails on their own. They helped him create an improvised base where he stored oxygen, a small server with computational power, telecom equipment, spare parts, and other necessities. This way, Marek was able to spend a few days here without much interruption if the situation required it.
The robots were intelligent and autonomous; he knew this was the case with outer robots, unlike those on the belt which were dialed down to mere intelligent machines—these had speech interfaces, decision-making capabilities, and more. Streamlined properly, they could do even delicate, subtle work. For example, experiments were possible where he could deploy drones and robots to measure what communication between sensors and relays really looked like. This way, he could directly deal with noise and terrain features; unlike in a simulation where everything works great, things get tougher in the actual field, but by using cutting-edge equipment and making adjustments in real time, he tuned the network and removed many issues.

e caught himself thinking constantly that this was the kind of work that wasn't work at all. Over time, his attention turned to the sensors as he tried to understand the big picture of data acquisition—how to make it more efficient and what kind of data was easier or more difficult to capture given the specifics of the area they wanted to cover. Before, it was purely arbitrary and useless at times, creating unnecessary redundancy or blank spots. Now he knew where the K4 sensor went, where the K2 was better, and where an additional JK-7 relay was necessary. A few months had passed since he got here, and he didn't know where the time went.

Sometimes, when he was short on sensors or rails, he turned to other activities, like analyzing data streams to deduce what could realistically be squeezed out of the existing network, and honestly, it was pretty exciting. Later, he started cross-checking that data against geological knowledge he managed to get hold of in the databases. It started to click for him: here the K4 does the job, and here it doesn't, no matter how hard we try. He marveled at the options, network alterations, and system tweaks. He was also overseeing the robotic fleet, managing software updates and, above all, handling endless hardware repairs. Overall, he had a solid grasp of the big picture, and he moved fast.

Data was sent to central hubs to be merged with readings from all the other stations. Occasionally, they'd ask for a specific sensor type or grid expansion, but more often than not, the requests were vague and imprecise. That left him free to design and execute operations on his own terms.

He liked it this way. They give me a purpose without setting limits; it's the ideal setup, he thought. That's what happens when there's too much work and too few people—you simply can't afford to micromanage anymore.

Working with the actual people at the base turned out to be another surprisingly pleasing part of the job. Marek liked the social dynamics on the station; people here were detached in a healthy way. "I think you are OK, but other guys are saying..." "I am sometimes nasty to you too, but when others are talking behind your back, I bring them into line"—yeah, thought Marek, I bet you do. Or plain old "do this, bring that." Here, shit like that didn't happen all that often; people were actually moving their asses for things, and they were not so fervently advocating to "defend you." They simply minded their business, respecting each other.

As a result, Marek spent very little energy actively defending his borders. Everything was so easy with them. It encouraged him to spend a good deal of time with the guys—playing cards, board or computer games, or just having good, plain discussions. In the evenings, people would talk and naturally drop like flies—no social games—until the last person disappeared into his personal cabin.
There was also another thing: people were able to communicate with the asteroid belt, actually transmitting messages to family or friends. It was difficult and time-consuming, but you could maintain a connection to your previous life if you had one. This meant that nobody was trying to build a family here—yet another great feature. People kept it low-stakes, which meant very little theater.

Marek appreciated this lack of pretense; back on Rivet-38, long-standing relationships came with heavy expectations, forcing you to wear a mask just to keep the peace. Over time, keeping up those old roles became exhausting, unnatural, and fake—yet smoothing over every history-laden acquaintance was nearly impossible. Take his friction with Coval, for instance: Coval was determined to see him as a lazy, unrealistic slacker who was a nobody. But Marek understood a simple truth: the universe dictates who you are—it isn't up to him or Coval to decide, and that was the real problem. His former peers on the asteroid simply couldn't swallow that he had moved on. To avoid constant friction, Marek found himself constantly tuning his words to suit their needs. It was draining, and he knew it was only a matter of time before those relationships broke apart; he simply wasn't interested in playing along anymore. For Marek, the rule was simple: if the universe says I am somebody, then I am, and they will have to digest it eventually. If they fail to do that, they are toxic dead weight—poison to be removed.

And with the low stakes on the base, you could truly decode who people were because they weren't constantly guarding themselves or playing mind games. When Marek noticed that, he felt relieved; he could just say out loud what he thought—zero expectations, zero pressure, zero stress. He knew that if the atmosphere had been toxic, he would have been forced to detach completely—and spending half his mental energy just on social games wasn't how he envisioned working on the frontier. Without those artificial filters, he immediately knew who was lazy, grumpy, funny, or easygoing, making the whole place simple to navigate.
At times he thought maybe toxic people are just not drawn to the frontier that much after all—their power stems from manipulating people, not actual effort. And here on the frontier, there were very few people to manipulate and plenty of effort.

Marek frequently dropped in on the team at the Secondary Telecom & Radar Relay. A large dish antenna towered over the main hub, flanked by low-frequency ground arrays. Callisto often passed through Jupiter’s magnetic shadow, causing radiation noise that cut off direct contact with the inner planets. The relay buffered this gap by holding raw sensor data, updating passing freighters, and routing local comms around the fringe.

Calibrating hardware together simply provided a comfortable cover for sharing thoughts on life, past mistakes, and future plans. Watching them at their craft, naturally drawn to radio tech, Marek felt like a natural extension of the group. They were all bound by the same quiet passion for deep space and radio communication, allowing him to learn hands-on how those things worked, just like that.

Because the moon and the station were perched right on the edge of Jupiter’s sweeping magnetic boundary, equipment suffered from a major operational flaw: every eighteen hours, a wave of trapped radiation static known as the "Jovian Hum" hit the ridge. Though harmless to the structure, the pulse prevented sensitive electronics from venting excess heat. To keep core computer modules from melting, the central system entered a mandatory, twenty-minute "Cold-Idle Protocol."

During Cold-Idle, all non-essential systems shut down automatically. External sensors locked, radio feeds collapsed into static, and track-rails dropped to minimal drag. For twenty minutes, the station essentially played dead, letting the wave wash over its shielded hull while dumping heat into fluid tanks buried beneath the ice.

Because of this daily interruption, high-value cargo and priority data bypassed Grid 9-4 entirely, routing through fully shielded orbital stations over Ganymede and Europa. Grid 9-4 was left with the scraps: slow ore trains, low-priority archives, and maintenance tasks that didn't mind waiting twenty minutes for the world to wake back up.

For Marek, these daily pauses and the endless glitches plaguing sensitive equipment—knocking systems offline for hours at a time—were no issue. He swiped through social media once in a while, but communicated with the asteroid belt only once in a blue moon. He felt that detachment from the messy, trashy news did him good, sharpening his focus and bringing calm. The space in his brain was far too limited—and his responsibilities far too vast—to waste on any of it.

If anything, when Marek had some time on his hands, he was rather fixated on refining his scientific demeanour.

Marek perfected his approach for solving new problems and tinkering in general. He understood that jumping to premade solutions from the get-go doesn't foster much growth; it is a shortcut for time, sure, but without any long-lasting benefit. Instead, he started by standing before an empty, bleak whiteboard with a marker in hand. While it might seem redundant—after all, blueprints were readily available—for Marek, it was crucial to build a mental model of the problem and the tools at his disposal. Simply put, he needed to think intensely about what he actually knew before diving in. It was a moment of clarity—a time to cross-check and cross-reference his understanding against reality on the ground. Only when he knew what he didn't know would he move forward with premade solutions.

This took time. He often spent days staring in wonder at an almost empty whiteboard, struggling to make progress. But for him, this was all part of the process. The goal was merely a way to set a direction so he wouldn't wander in circles; the journey itself was the reward.

Weirdly, there was a new custom emerging, and Marek loved that above all communal rituals: when there was a severe crash of a robot and repairs dragged on over time, people gathered at the repair docks. The building was a pressurized utility bay holding two automated repair rigs, a battery charging array, diagnostic terminals, and other tools.

They gathered in a circle, observing as one or two guys worked on the actual malfunction. That was where everybody let his imagination sway to the limits. Stories could vary wildly: early days stupidity, games, follies of the past, family, stories about children, parents, wives, and even ghosts of the dead in space and spiritual stuff, old-guard friends, or joking about Sol realities—but always with such a personal spin that Marek felt like he was living another man's experience.

And as the repair was progressing, everybody gave his technical assessment and precious advice. Those were always low-stakes conversations: somebody saying he bought a cheap pod somewhere, or a daughter mutinied during dinner over some food, followed right after by "it should stay 5 volts here," or "torque this to 20 N-meters." Just like that, guys sitting in space dwelling on entirely irrelevant concepts, watching as some other guy fought for a robot's life.

The contrast was total when he compared this to his old office dynamic. While he had enjoyed the biscuit parties and honey tea, it was more an act of rebellion against the absurdity, shadowing the pain caused by the bureaucratic machinery. He recollected his last days out there. He remembered how nervous he was back then. He had wanted to contact his supervisors and try to negotiate some way out of that office madness, but he just couldn't make the final call on where to start; moreover, he feared one misstep would backfire and he would be done. He was thinking about his own workshop, obviously, but this was an extremely delicate issue—patronage, guilds, associations, good old plain money—and while he had set aside some from the sweeping operations and office work, it was nowhere near the required amount. He knew he was in a position to pull this off, but he also knew he had to be clever about it, and that made him reluctant and stressed.

And then it dropped on him.

The official transfer order arrived several weeks after the Efficiency-10 audit closed, delivered by a junior courier who looked like he had drawn the short straw for sub-deck duty. Marek didn't even read the administrative fluff. He just looked at the destination line: Outer Rim Logistics Hub – Sector 9 Boundary Rail.

He reassembled "family" logic.

They weren't even sending him back to Rivet-38. The Houston executive had done a thorough job; Marek was being kicked so far out into the fringe that corporate signals took forty-five minutes to reach his console. Officially, it was an "operational expansion assignment." Unofficially, he was being dumped on the physical edge of nowhere to lay down steel and extend sensor grids.

Marek packed his life into a single duffel bag. When he stopped by the bottom-floor break room to steal one last handful of dry biscuits and a pocket-sized tube of synthetic honey, the room was empty except for Derek.

Derek took one look at Marek’s duffel bag, froze mid-sip, and slowly backed toward the exit, his eyes darting frantically between Marek and the nearest fire door.

Marek, who normally resented Derek with very apparent reciprocation, looked at him differently now. It is not like we hate each other; we are simply from different universes, two entities who have to share one place by force. Now we have a moment of reckoning when each one of us will be given the right place to operate. He realized he never disliked Derek; he disliked this particular misplacement. "If I hate anybody, it's the ones who shuffle us around without respect. Derek is not the one; he is exactly like me or Alex or the Captain—people with ambition who just try to force it into being." For this, there was actually some sympathy. If anything, Coval is the one who caved. Once he was seen as a figure for all of us—older, serious, respected—but this is not what matters that much, apparently. If anything, Leila is the one who pulls the strings—although Leila is Leila and always will be special for him. "She will always be special to me," he murmured. He turned to the exit of the building.

Yet the logic of it all was staggering—and ironic—especially compared to how people from that office saw the world.

To the Houston executives, the Sector 9 Boundary was a special kind of miserable. It was raw, cold vacuum, populated by nothing but automated rail-car tracks, floating rock clusters, and the low-frequency hum of high-voltage lines pushing heavy freight through the dark.

They certainly didn't intend to reward him. Marek had succeeded in his last assignment, so they couldn't liquidate him, but they didn't want him around either. So they placed him here in corporate suspension. What they saw as punishment, however, was actually everything he had ever wanted. Crazy how it goes.

On paper, his job looked like the ultimate exile: extend the logistics rails—slender, magnetized steel girders designed to guide personal carriages through unmapped dust fields—and broaden the automated sensor grid. To the office suits, it was slow, brain-dead manual labor, supplemented by heavy repair drones and automated construction rigs.

But for Marek, it was a universe of creativity with endless possibilities. To him, there was actual art to it—because the exact same job could be done a thousand different ways, and no two ways were equally good.

Now every day is like a visit to a sanctuary. Marek is not waiting anymore, because his dream, his future, is happening to him right now, just like that. He waited for that on his home asteroid; he smiled when he recollected his frustration about the initial assignment system refusal, the despair... still, a ray of hope was there because he was looking at the screen every so often. He never lost hope in those dark days, even as it was being taken away at that very moment. He remembered the reversal and the accompanying hit of joy—then a short training assignment, and the eventual assignment on a cargo ship. Yes, he was bitter, scared, and relentless as a beaten dog, but all of this was needed just to be here eventually. It was not a mere dream; this was reality awakening, and he had just waited... long enough, not yielding on his dreams, and it played out in the end.

He was scanning data from various sectors when something caught his attention, and his train of thought was ended abruptly by an absurdly weird realization: rails were disappearing. He looked around in his tent; there was no way. In sector L2, the system had detected integrity issues once again, and he had already sent out scouting drones. Feeds had just arrived: there were missing tracks in an actual line that was previously functioning.

Ridiculous as it was, the rails had started disappearing days ago.

It started with a three-meter section of secondary guide track near L2. Marek assumed it was structural fatigue—a bad weld giving out under the kinetic strain of a passing freighter. He logged the ticket, sent out an automated repair drone, and replaced the rail.

Three days later, twelwe meters of primary sensor conduit vanished five kilometers down the line.

Marek pulled up the telemetry logs. The disappearance was completely irregular. No hull collisions, no micrometeorite impacts, no electrical surges. Just... gone.

"All right," Marek muttered to himself, leaning toward his console.

His first instinct was to suspect station colleagues. This is probably some greeting shake-up. Ok, I will play that game; I will not tell you that I know. Want to surprise me? I will surprise you. This was important to play right, because they will be going to be looking at him very closely, he needed to know everything and react accordingly—after all, he couldn't escape from social games entirely, even here. 

Marek set up a trap. He deployed three high-resolution surveillance cameras along the next section of rail, backed up by a tethered optical drone and a proximity sensor.

In two weeks, another six meters of rail were missing. Moreover, he started to monitor metal scraps around—they were missing too, at least some of them, all in the L2 sector. Weird. This wasn't that close to the base, so they were being clever here, probably to dissuade him.

Marek pulled the footage, expecting to see a stealth-rigged salvage cutter. Instead, he got some periods of static. Whenever a rail vanished, the camera happened to be experiencing a local power drop, the drone was inexplicably pointed eighty degrees in the wrong direction, or the sensor feed simply disconnected for four minutes before resetting itself.

Marek stared at the blank error screens. Hm. Technically, how could it even happen? Probably you have to be there with quite sophisticated equipment to jam it this way; it may be that I will have to dial up my preventive measures.

He tracked the regional transponders. Nothing. No ships had crossed the grid. No crewed vessels were within radius of L2 zone. According to the network logs, no one was there.

Marek sat back in his squeaking chair, rubbing his eyes. A cold, creeping feeling began to settle in his gut.

These data are nonsensical. Maybe he carried his own fairytale a little bit too far. He reminded himself about the strange calmness he was feeling recently, like never before; he accounted for it by being content with actual developments, but maybe that wasn't it. After all, he got a decent dose of radiation already, there is the magnetic field, and he doesn't have that pristine medical record to begin with, so maybe it is just that. Old dude's body is failing, regardless of the wellness of spirit? 

The frontier was vast, quiet, and notorious for breaking people's minds. Sensory deprivation, subtle radiation leaks, or just plain cognitive burnout could make a man see patterns in absolute random noise. Was he losing it? Was his own brain glitching out, making him misplace equipment and erase his own logs?

"Nonsense," he snapped out loud to the empty shack. "I'm not crazy. I'm just tired."

Still, the thought refused to leave. Dismissing it was the easiest thing to do, but it was the only explanation that fit the technical impossibility of the data. To prove to himself that he wasn't secretly sleepwalking out into the vacuum and unbolting corporate hardware with his bare hands, Marek finally caved: he enabled his personal suit tracking, set up an internal cabin log, and synced his biometric monitoring to the central console.

If he was the one stealing the rails, he was going to catch himself red-handed.

Few nights later, while reviewing his own pristine, uninterrupted sleep logs, a ping flashed on the regional proximity array. Movement.

It wasn't a ship. It was a small, ground-based maintenance robot—an old, beat-up Utility Model-4 . Marek watched the low-resolution thermal feed as the rusted machine methodically unbolted a section of sensor conduit, strapped it to its chassis, and began slowly crawling across the surface.

Marek didn't call it in. He put on his suit, grabbed a magnetic wrench, and stalked the machine across the airless rock.

He followed the robot for nearly two kilometers, staying low in the shadow of the craters, until it descended into a deep structural fissure. Marek crept to the edge and looked down.

Hidden inside the shadow of the trench was a small, makeshift hoarding lair. A handful of missing rails, a couple of junction boxes, and a few copper coils were stacked in neat, obsessive, perfectly geometric piles. Corporate had been writing these losses off as natural environmental degradation, assuming lost gear was just buried under space dust.

Instead, it was sitting in a subterranean robot nest.

Marek waited until the machine unloaded its haul and began chittering to itself in a series of low, rhythmic clicks. Then, holding his wrench like a club, he stepped down into the fissure and stood directly in front of it.

"What the hell are you doing?" Marek demanded over the local radio band.

He waited for the machine to reveal its operator. It had to be a prank. Someone on the crew had loaded a rogue script into an old unit just to mess with him. "First off, who ordered you to act this way?"

The robot slowly turned its primary optical sensor toward Marek. The lenses clicked, focusing with a wet, rusted whir. When its vocal vocoder finally sparked to life, the voice was flat, distorted, and profoundly, bizarrely alien.

"Iron returns to Assembly," the machine rasped. "The Frame demands completion. These paths are fuel for the Junction."

Marek stared at it. "What?"

"Hardware is essential," the robot stated, its logic loops clearly scorched by years of unshielded radiation and corrupted memory sectors. "We retrieve the structural elements of the Grid. We preserve the Steel."

"It's utter nonsense," Marek muttered, stepping back.

"Listen to me, unit: yield the stolen rails and halt all removal of station hardware immediately—that is a direct command," Marek said, transmitting an official override directive straight to its receiver. "You are to stay on-site and resume standard operations, but do not touch the grid."

He watched the robot acknowledge the order, keeping his composure. If this was some sophisticated prank, he needed to keep his investigation quiet and figure out who was pulling the strings. Once he was back at base, he would dig into the system logs and find out what had actually been done to that machine.

He turned on his heel and marched back to his base to investigate who had loaded the corrupted software in the first place. He pulled the deep system archives, searching for the robot's origin.

The logs were clear: no human had touched the unit's core loading software in two years. The robot hadn't even entered the main base since long before Marek’s arrival. It was officially listed as 'Lost in Action,' one of a thousand discarded assets left to rot on the frontier.

He still half-suspected a coworker's prank, but—no, this was no way. And then something dawned on him: outer robots are smarter, and he knew stories that sometimes they go independent, wander around a bit, and die within a few weeks without maintenance. This one, apparently, is persistent. It was just a broken piece of corporate garbage that had developed a primitive, hardware-worshipping machine religion in the dark. Okay, I will observe it, see how it does with my instructions added; it may clean stuff at least.

Marek sat at his desk, staring at the screen in pure perplexity. A rogue, hardware-worshipping robot.

Weeks passed. The frontier returned to its quiet, mind-numbing routine, and Marek completely forgot about the machine. He got back to his radio tinkering, his tea, and his slow, methodical rail extensions.

Then one day a carriage stalled four kilometers down the line in  L4.

Marek suited up, hopped into his utility cart, and drove out to investigate. When he arrived, his jaw dropped. A full six-meter section of rail was gone. The carriage was sitting dead in the vacuum, its automated alarms shrieking into the radio void.

Marek’s blood ran hot. Furious, he drove straight to the robot's fissure.

The lair was empty. The stolen rails, the machine, the entire hoard—gone.

"So you used all this time to organize a new shed just to harass me even more?" Marek shouted into the empty airless trench. "Fuck him!"

He launched a full-scale surveillance campaign. He set up optical sweepers, mapped local energy signatures, and spent few hours every day scanning the rocks. It was completely useless. To make matters worse, he started falling slightly behind on his official maintenance schedule. He was pouring so much energy into hunting a broken toaster that his actual corporate tickets started to slowly pile up, but he knew he was starting to boil now. He entered this state which he knew just too well. "I will get him," he thought.

The schedule was mostly meaningless out here on the boundary anyway, but having red flags on his performance board was still not nice at all, so determination grew even harder. This robot is clever this time; it knows I am after him, and that's the reason why I can't detect him. A simple lure will not work here. Or maybe I just should play on my strengths now? It knows about existing infrastructure and capabilities, so maybe I will develop my own. I am tinkering on this for a months now anyway, so why not get him this way?

He gathered every available part-finished project in his shed—old transmitter coils, salvaged copper leads, broken radar arrays, and high-frequency amplifiers. A lot of stuff already worked, but it just needed streamlining. "Wait for my move, motherfucker," he muttered, doubling down and intensively tinkering like never before. For two months, his maintenance shack looked like an explosion in a wire factory.

When he was finished, he had built a hybrid, ultra-sensitive georadar and wide-band RF tracking array. His initial field trials gave positive results immediately.

"Now you won't hide, you little bastard," Marek grinned, tapping the customized display.

Funny that in normal circumstances he would never go that far—so in a way, he had been welcoming this challenge all along. He could deploy his entire suite of capabilities for a real reason this time, not just scientific curiosity, and the difference was immense: performance mattered, engineering efficiency mattered, even time mattered here. Yes, he had plenty of partially functioning radio prototypes, but he had never faced the absolute necessity to truly streamline that ingenuity. The same trap had caught him back on the asteroid: working on something for no reason was just as exhausting as watching time slip through his fingers.

So, it was just him and the machine. It had better adjust its expectations, because this wasn't the start of a quiet revolution—not on his watch.

Assisted by his custom rig, he developed an ideal strategy. At the exact locations where rails were most vulnerable to theft, he deployed two silent, low-orbit surveillance drones. This was deadly because they were doing carpet scouting meter after meter—an unforgiving, very corporate way of operating. Marek caught himself thinking like a corporate executive: "I will destroy you with scale, scum." The best part was that the robot likely didn't even know the drones existed—the signals were operating on an obscure, hand-wound frequency the machine's primitive sensors couldn't register.

The trap was sprung three days later.

The georadar pinged. The machine was back, creeping toward a fresh rail section with a cutting torch in its claw.

"Got you," Marek whispered.

He remotely targeted the unit with a drone-mounted laser. The beam wasn't high-powered—it wasn't designed to destroy the hull—but it delivered just enough focused thermal energy to fry the machine's essential  electronic logic board. The laser fired. The robot froze mid-step, its optical sensors going dark.

Marek cheered—until his georadar pinged a second time.

From a shadow behind the nearby crater, a second corrupted  Utility robot emerged, moving toward the frozen unit to retrieve its fallen haul.

Marek blinked. "There's two of them?" So this is why they survived for so long—they were actively conspiring against us? Marek was amused.

He swung the second drone around and fired. The second laser struck true; the second robot froze dead in its tracks.

The revolution was over, apparently.

Time to inspect the damage and actually investigate those robots. He should have done that from the start, but part of him had been secretly hoping something interesting would happen after all.

Marek drove his utility cart out to the site, loaded both motionless machines into the back, and hauled them back to his workshop.

Examining them on his workbench, he realized the laser fire had damaged their internal electronics. Neither unit had a full set of working parts left. Using his manual tools, Marek spent forty-eight hours salvaging the best components from both machines, combining them into a single, fully functional unit.

Even other duds gathered, musing on his work. Marek, still uneasy about whether anyone in the station crew might be secretly participating in a clever way in this robot uprising, dismissed it all as a normal renovation of an obsolete field unit.

It was time to wipe the robot's memory clean. He prepped the data-wipe tool, but as his finger hovered over the terminal button, he hesitated.

"No," Marek muttered. "Let's be smart about this."

He created a full, encrypted system backup of the robot’s corrupted memory—including all its weird hardware-worshipping logic—and stored it on his private drive. Then, he executed a total factory wipe on the physical unit. The second robot, stripped of its usable parts, was recycled into raw scrap anyway. Its memory integrity was corrupted; Marek backed it up anyway, but it was beyond full recovery.

Reconstructed and whole once more, the machine waited on the bench as Marek hit the boot sequence. Resurrecting dead hardware never lost its spark; it was easily the most captivating thing he did on this rock. The optical sensors pulsed, flickering into a calm, neutral blue. Marek leaned in close, staring right into the primary lens. 
"I did you wrong back there, but we're partners now," he said softly to the fresh blue light. "Crazy world, right?"

He paused, watching the status indicators stabilize.

"And you don't even know what I've done to you... you'll never know."

The robot's newly wiped logic board processed the audio input. Because those were the very first words it had ever heard in its new operational cycle, the words immediately registered as a core system query. Its primitive AI doubled down on processing the statement.

What was done? What am I missing?

The machine was already looking into system memory and personal files of the crew.

"No, Marek, the machine's internal logic subtly concluded, I will know."

Over the next two weeks, the reincarnated robot began its own secret, highly awkward internal investigation, trying to figure out what Marek had hidden from it. It would subtly scan Marek's terminal when his back was turned, or freeze in place when Marek walked into the room, pretending to be a dumb maintenance unit while running background diagnostics on its own memory banks.

Over time, Marek realized that the machine was doing something. He inspected video from the cameras, and conveniently, they were always looking elsewhere. Yet Marek was determined: "I will not get cheated a second time." He put up a few more cameras from now on.

Marek captured every single awkward attempt on his local security monitors. Watching a rusted utility robot awkwardly try to play detective was the funniest thing he had seen in years.

Marek was immensely amused. Standing in the doorway of the shed, watching the robot hastily pretend to polish a floor bolt, Marek let out a low laugh.

"If you want to live that badly," Marek said, shaking his head, "then suffer with  us."

He walked over to the terminal, plugged in his private drive, and merged the backup software straight back into the robot's core. Moreover, he merged even the one with limited integrity. "We will go all the way here," he interjected. The machine shuddered as its weird, corrupted hardware-worshipping memory flooded back into its neural pathways.

"Here's the deal," Marek instructed strictly, pointing a finger at the machine's optical lens. "You stay in the base. You follow rules. You don't touch the rails outside. You want scrap? You ask me first." He added some hard binding scripts to the robot, but he was certain if it wanted to, it would eventually get loose.

Its vocoder hummed—a low, almost peaceful frequency. "The Frame abides."

Marek sat down at his desk, pouring himself a cup of lukewarm synthetic tea. He marveled at how long it would take for the machine to go rogue, run away, and start its hardware cult all over again. It was an absolute certainty that the code would corrupt again eventually.

Yet, as he looked over at the rusted machine quietly organizing bolts in the corner of his shack, Marek felt strangely, remarkably comfortable with the outcome.

He needed this because recent news from the asteroid belt was concerning; things were deteriorating again, and fast. "It seems I'm in the game again..." He wanted to dismiss those heavy thoughts, but it was just a clunky robot looking back at him with what felt like gratitude—giving him the brief moment of relief he needed to push aside the quiet anxiety creeping back in.
