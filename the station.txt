


The Boundary Rail Hub at Grid 9-4 wasn’t a grand orbital habitat or a bustling transit port. It was a glorified secondary relay—a cold, low-mass outpost bolted directly into the cracked crust of Callisto’s outermost ice-and-rock ridge. In the cold hierarchy of corporate logistics, the station barely registered as a named asset on spreadsheets. It existed solely as a low-capacity bridge to anchor local magnetic guide-rails, gather backup sensor data, and catch faint deep-space signals that main relays deemed too low-priority to process.

Operating the station was an exercise in automated persistence, structured around three main systems.

First were the Logistical Rail Anchor Systems. The station maintained a twenty-kilometer stretch of ground-based magnetic guide-rails that steered uncrewed heavy ore sleds through Callisto's cratered terrain toward equatorial cargo docks. Power came from a small, buried atomic generator, sending steady magnetic fields through the track to keep cargo carts aligned against the moon's uneven gravity.

There was also an auxiliary, simplified personal railway to facilitate scientific activities—placing and maintaining a network of thousands of various sensors spread miles in various areas. Marek got a quite exciting assignment: he was planning and executing end-to-end development of those rails alongside sensor positioning and maintaining. Initially, Marek couldn't believe his eyes when he was given this responsibility, but here, where the crew of the station was sparse and the priority of the facility was low, they could not afford to mess up with various committees. He surveyed the equipment in the warehouse and the current state of the network; it was decent, but immediately he spotted potential for development, and there was plenty of room for improvement with white holes in the network. He started right away to learn how to operate the robots, carts, systems, and planning software. The work was staggering in scope, but he was not feeling faint-hearted this time. After a few weeks, he was on the actual edge with his robots placing railways and brooding over sensory networking protocol configuration, analyzing radio emission schedules, noise parameters, and interferences. At times, while watching the robots unpacking rails, he was brooding over the trip to this place. 

He got here on an actual passenger ship, although most of the way he was put to sleep; overall, for one month he was walking around the hull. Not initially at first—his access was limited. He obviously didn't understand how it all worked, but man, this was like 100 years in the future—everything so well thought out, brilliant design. He knew that passenger ships were this way, but the contrast to the cargo vessel was staggering.

The issue was random endless logs obscuring information and important details about the problem, and his understanding of the system was nonexistent. Obviously, his favorite part was just walking around the ship and trying to put together the functionalities of components.

When the travel was about to conclude, the maintenance chief with emphasized surprise noted that he couldn't solve such a trivial problem. Marek silently admitted defeat, but overall he was equally satisfied. The alternative would be to stay in the passenger quarters for the entire journey.

Despite its simple design, the station suffered from a major operational problem caused by its position on the ridge. Perched right on the edge of Jupiter’s sweeping magnetic boundary, the station was hit every eighteen hours by a wave of trapped radiation static known locally as the "Jovian Hum."

While the wave was too weak to cause structural damage, it was a nightmare for sensitive electronics because the station couldn't vent excess heat properly during the pulse. To keep core computer modules from melting, the central system was programmed to enter a mandatory, twenty-minute "Cold-Idle Protocol."

When Cold-Idle kicked in, all non-essential systems shut down automatically. External sensors locked into place, radio feeds collapsed into static, and the track-rails dropped to minimal passive magnetic drag. Because of this daily interruption, no priority corporate traffic ever relied on Grid 9-4. High-value cargo and priority data streams were routed through fully shielded orbital stations over Ganymede and Europa. Grid 9-4 was left with the scraps: slow ore trains, low-priority archives, and forgotten maintenance tasks that didn't mind waiting twenty minutes for the world to wake back up.

Marek sat down at his desk, pouring himself a cup of lukewarm synthetic tea. He marveled at how long it would take for the machine to go rogue, run away, and start its hardware cult all over again. It was an absolute certainty that the code would corrupt again eventually.

Yet, as he looked over at the rusted machine quietly organizing bolts in the corner of his shack, Marek felt strangely, remarkably comfortable with the outcome.

He needed this because recent news on the asteroid belt gave him chills; it is going south again, and quick. "It seems I am in the game," and this robot gave him a brief moment of joy he needed to overcome the existential dread which started to creep in again.
